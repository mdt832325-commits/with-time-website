The Future

The City That Will Outlive Us All

Every street you walk was once somebody's impossible plan. The city you live in is a letter from the dead to the unborn.

With Time · November 15, 2026 · 6 min read

Walk through any city and count the builders you will never meet. Somebody planned this road. Somebody dug this drain. Somebody planted the tree that shades your walk to the shop — a person whose name nobody remembers, who planted something knowing they would never sit under it. Every street you walk was once somebody's impossible plan, argued about in rooms that no longer exist, built by hands that are now dust. The city is the only book the dead ever finished writing.

Now imagine this city in the year 2126. A hundred years from now. What will still be here? The river, certainly — the river was here before the city and will be here after it. The old market square, probably, because people have always needed a place to meet and buy and argue. The school, maybe, with different children in the same classrooms. And the things you think are permanent — the towers, the flyovers, the shining new mall — those are the most temporary of all. Cities keep what they love and quietly demolish what they only needed.

Here is the strange part: you are building that future city right now, without knowing it. The road being repaired outside your window, the tree being planted on the corner, the classroom being painted — someone in 2126 will walk past them without a thought, the way you walk past the work of people from 1926. Your present is somebody's past, and somebody's future is your present. We are all construction workers on a building we will never see finished.

The future is not a place we travel to. It is a place we build for strangers. That sounds sad until you realise it is the kindest thing humans do. The person who laid the first stone of a school did not do it for themselves. The person who dug the first well did not drink from it for a hundred years. We build for people we will never meet, and we do it without thinking, because somewhere deep in us we understand that a city is not property. It is a promise.

A child born today will see the year 2126. She will walk streets you walked, drink water from pipes you paid for, sit under trees you never planted. What will she thank you for? Not the tallest building, probably. Not the fastest road. More likely the small things: the park that still has shade, the library that still opens its doors, the river that still runs clean. The future does not remember what was impressive. It remembers what was cared for.

So plant something that takes a hundred years to grow. It does not have to be a tree — though a tree is a good choice. It can be a habit, a school, a kindness, a road kept clean. Do it knowing you will never see the end of it. That is not a tragedy. That is the whole point. The city will outlive us all, and that is exactly why it is worth building well.

“We do not inherit the city from our ancestors. We borrow it from our grandchildren.”
