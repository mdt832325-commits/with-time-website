One Million Satellites in Orbit

The FCC has authorized SpaceX to launch one million satellites — not for internet, but as data centers in space. The cloud is moving above the clouds.

With Time · November 27, 2026 · 5 min read

On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the United States Federal Communications Commission did something no regulator has ever done before. It gave SpaceX permission to put one million satellites into orbit — not to beam internet down to Earth, but to think up there. The satellites, called orbital compute modules, would form a constellation named Starmind: a data center in the sky, running on sunlight, processing the world’s artificial intelligence above the weather, above the clouds, above everything.

One million is a number that refuses to sit still in the mind. Right now, there are only about seventeen thousand satellites in orbit around Earth — every nation, every company, every purpose, all together. Starmind alone would be nearly sixty times that. The authorization, filed as FCC order DA 26-1273, allows the satellites to fly in shells between 500 and 1,002 kilometres up, tilted at thirty degrees and in Sun-synchronous orbits, so that they pass over the same places at the same time of day, again and again, like a slow tide of machines.

The schedule is long, and it is written into the license like a promise. The first Starmind satellite must be in orbit by November 2033. A hundred thousand of them — ten percent — by 2035. Half a million by 2038. The full million by 2040. SpaceX’s chief financial officer has said the company plans to fly its first orbital data centers as early as 2027, and to keep expanding through 2029. The idea, the FCC said, is to meet the “rapidly growing demand” for AI computing power — and to do it with solar power, in a place where the Sun never sets behind a hill and no river is needed to cool the machines.

And yet the announcement landed with a question mark as large as the number itself. Scientists are skeptical, and some are alarmed. A million new lights in the sky would transform the night — astronomers already struggle to photograph the stars through the trains of satellites we have today. And SpaceX itself, in a filing ahead of its planned public offering in June, cautioned that launching data centers in space relied on “unproven” technologies and “may not achieve commercial viability.” The company that wants to build it is telling you, in its own paperwork, that it might not work.

That is the strange shape of the future sometimes: a plan so large it sounds like science fiction, approved by a government agency, doubted by the scientists, and hedged by its own builders — all in the same week. If it works, the computers that run our lives will no longer sit in warehouses on the ground. They will circle overhead, a million of them, catching sunlight and doing our thinking in the dark between the stars. Your photos, your messages, your questions to a machine — answered from orbit.

We have spent a century moving our machines farther from us — from the desk to the room to the building to the warehouse at the edge of town. Now the next address is not a place on Earth at all. It is a shell of light, five hundred kilometres up, where a million satellites may one day hum quietly above our heads, holding the cloud in the sky it was named after.

“America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence.” — Brendan Carr, Chairman of the FCC
Your turn

If the computers that run your life moved into orbit — a million of them circling overhead — would you trust them with your memories?
