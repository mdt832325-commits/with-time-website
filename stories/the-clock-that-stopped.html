Time Itself

The Clock That Stopped at 3:57

In so many houses there is a clock that no longer runs — and nobody will fix it, and nobody will throw it away. Because it is not broken. It is keeping a different kind of time.

With Time · October 24, 2026 · 5 min read

It hangs in the hallway of the old house, or above a mantel, or in the kitchen of someone you love: a clock whose hands have not moved in years. 3:57, in this telling — though in your family's telling it might be 6:12 or 11:40. Someone could fix it for the price of a sandwich. Nobody does. The clock is not broken, exactly. It has been promoted. It no longer tells the time; it tells a time.

Every stopped clock has a story standing behind it like a held breath. Sometimes the story is gentle: the clock belonged to a grandfather, and when he died, nobody could bring themselves to wind it, and a week of stillness became a decade. Sometimes it is sharper: the power failed during the storm, the quake, the night of the phone call — and when the lights came back, the clock simply never rejoined the present. Families rarely decide these things. They just notice, one day, that the clock stopped on the day everything else did, and they leave it that way on purpose without ever saying so.

There is a famous old line that a stopped clock is right twice a day, usually offered as a joke about uselessness. Families who keep one hear it differently. Twice a day, at 3:57, the working clocks of the world come around to agree with it — the whole hurrying planet briefly synchronized with the moment the old clock is keeping. Twice a day, for one minute, it is not behind the times. The times are with it.

Objects like this are how households actually practice history. Museums have plaques; families have clocks, and chipped bowls, and chairs nobody sits in. They are not superstition. They are storage. A stopped clock stores an hour the way a photograph stores a face — imperfectly, faithfully, and within reach of everyone who passes it in the hall. Children grow up reading it long before they understand it, which is exactly the order in which family history is supposed to arrive.

Should you wind yours, if you have one? That is genuinely your family's call, and there is no wrong answer. Some families restart the clock at a birth or a wedding, letting it carry the new hour forward on the old one's shoulders. Some let it rest, and buy a cheap digital for the kitchen, and find the arrangement perfectly sane. Time, after all, is not in short supply in a house. What is scarce — what the stopped clock guards — is the moment.

So if you pass one this week, in your hallway or someone else's, spare it a glance at 3:57, whatever it says. It is not failing at its job. It finished its job, on a particular afternoon, and has been standing watch over the minute ever since — the most loyal employee the house ever had.

“A stopped clock is not wrong. It is a photograph that learned to hang on a wall.”
