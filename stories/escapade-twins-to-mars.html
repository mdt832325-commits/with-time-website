The Two Little Probes Racing to Mars

Blue and Gold are each about the size of a washing machine, and they are about to do something no small spacecraft has ever done: ride a slingshot around Earth and sail on to Mars.

With Time · November 24, 2026 · 4 min read

On the 7th of November, a small spacecraft called Blue left Earth. Two days later, its twin, Gold, followed. Each one is about the size of a washing machine. Each one weighs less than a grand piano. And together, the two of them are about to do something no spacecraft their size has ever done: swing around the Earth like a stone from a sling, and let our planet's gravity throw them all the way to Mars.

The mission is called ESCAPADE, and it is NASA's bet that small can be mighty. For decades, the road to Mars belonged to big, expensive orbiters. Blue and Gold are different — two identical twins, built to fly in formation, watching Mars with two sets of eyes instead of one. They will arrive in orbit around the Red Planet in September 2027, and then they will get to work on a mystery that has haunted us for a century.

The mystery is this: Mars once had rivers, lakes, maybe even an ocean — and then it lost its sky. The solar wind, a constant stream of charged particles from the Sun, slowly stripped the Martian air away, molecule by molecule, until the rivers dried and the planet turned to desert. Earth survived the same wind because we have a magnetic field, an invisible shield that turns the wind aside. Mars's shield died long ago.

Blue and Gold will measure exactly how the wind steals a planet's air — one probe watching the wind as it arrives, the other watching the atmosphere as it escapes, both at the same moment. It is the kind of measurement that takes two spacecraft, flying in step, hundreds of millions of miles from home.

There is something quietly brave about the plan. The slingshot around Earth has to be perfect — a fraction of a degree wrong and the twins miss Mars entirely. But if it works, the smallest mission ever sent to another planet will answer one of the biggest questions we have: how does a world lose its sky — and how did ours manage to keep it?

“Mars lost its sky to the wind. Two little probes are going to find out exactly how.”
