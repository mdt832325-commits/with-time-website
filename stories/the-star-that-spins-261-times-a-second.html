The Star That Spins 261 Times a Second

PSR J1622-7204 is a dead star the size of a city, spinning faster than a kitchen blender — and it took fifteen years of data and a brand-new kind of mathematics to finally hear it.

With Time · November 24, 2026 · 5 min read

There is a star out there that spins 261 times every second. Not 261 times a minute, or an hour — a second. In the time it takes you to blink, it has turned around more than a hundred times. It is called PSR J1622-7204, and it is a pulsar: the corpse of a massive star, squeezed down to the size of a city, spinning like a lighthouse beam that sweeps past Earth 261 times a second.

Finding it took fifteen years. The Fermi space telescope watched the sky for a decade and a half, collecting gamma rays — the most energetic kind of light — and somewhere in that mountain of data was a faint, regular ticking: the pulsar's pulse, hidden in the noise. The problem was the search itself. Checking every possible spin, every possible wobble, the old way, would have taken about six hundred years.

So astronomers built a better way to look. Using a mathematical tool called a lattice — a kind of clever shortcut through the possibilities — they cut the search from six centuries to about ten hours. Ten hours, for fifteen years of sky. The paper announcing the discovery appeared in Physical Review Letters on the 19th of November, 2026, and it is as much a story about a new way of seeing as it is about the star itself.

Pulsars like this one are born in violence and kept spinning by theft. Long ago, this dead star stole gas from a companion star, and the stolen gas spun it up like a top, faster and faster, until it reached a speed no human machine could ever survive. It has been spinning ever since, ticking with the precision of an atomic clock — so precise that astronomers use pulsars like it to test Einstein's theories and to listen for ripples in spacetime itself.

Think about the patience in this story. Fifteen years of watching. Six hundred years of searching, folded into ten hours by a new idea. A star that has spun 261 times a second, unnoticed, for longer than human beings have had telescopes. It was always there, ticking. We just had to learn how to listen.

“The universe keeps its fastest secrets in the faintest signals — and patience is the only way to hear them.”
