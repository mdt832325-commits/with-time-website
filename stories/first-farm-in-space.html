The First Farm in Space

On Sunday, November 29, 2026, a rocket lifts off from Cape Canaveral carrying eleven thousand pounds of cargo — and hidden inside it, a small chamber that could change how humans eat on the Moon and Mars.

With Time · November 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Tomorrow, a rocket launches carrying a farm. Not a metaphor, and not a science-fiction prop: a real, working growth chamber, built to grow real food, on its way to orbit. The launch is Northrop Grumman’s NG-25 cargo mission, a SpaceX Falcon 9 lifting off from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral no earlier than 10:47 in the morning Eastern time on Sunday, November 29, 2026. Riding on top is Cygnus XL — a larger, stretched version of the Cygnus cargo ship, named S.S. James “Jim” A. Lovell after the Apollo 13 commander — carrying about eleven thousand pounds of supplies, experiments and food for the International Space Station.

And tucked among that cargo is Ohalo III. It is a prototype growth chamber from the company Ohalo, and it is the first operational crop-production system ever sent to space. That is the secret in the hold: everything else on the manifest keeps the station running for a few more months, but this one small chamber is a test of whether astronauts can grow their own food on the way to somewhere much farther away.

Space farming is not new, exactly. Astronauts aboard the station have been growing lettuce, chillies and tomatoes for years in small experiment units with names like Veggie and the Advanced Plant Habitat — careful, hand-tended science, a few leaves at a time. Ohalo III is a different step. It is designed not as an experiment but as a production system: a chamber meant to grow food at scale, reliably, with the kind of automation a small crew on a long voyage would actually need. If it works, it is the prototype for the greenhouses that will feed crews on the Moon and on Mars, where no supply ship can arrive in two days and every meal has to come from the ship itself.

Getting there is a careful piece of choreography. After launch, Cygnus XL will chase the station for about two days. On Tuesday, December 1, astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney will catch it with the station’s robotic arm, Canadarm2, and berth it to the station. The ship will then stay docked until May 2027 — five months of deliveries, experiments and, for the first time, a working farm among the cargo.

Think about what that means for a crew on the way to Mars. A trip there takes six to nine months each way, and every kilogram of food launched from Earth costs fuel, money and room that could hold something else. A farm changes the arithmetic: seeds weigh almost nothing, water can be recycled, and light can be made with electricity from solar panels. The plants do the rest — turning light, water and air into food, and giving the crew something no packaged meal can: something green, something alive, something that grew where they are.

There is a quieter reason, too. Astronauts who have grown plants in orbit talk about it the same way every time: the smell of a tomato plant in a metal box, the sight of green against the black outside the window, the small daily ritual of tending something that is growing. On a voyage measured in months, that may matter as much as the calories.

So the rocket going up tomorrow is not only carrying supplies. It is carrying the first page of a new chapter — the one where humans stop packing every meal for the journey and start growing dinner on the way. A farm, in space, at last.
