One Light-Day Away

Voyager 1 has become the first human-made object ever to reach one light-day from Earth — 16.1 billion miles away. A signal sent today takes a full day to get there, and another full day to come back.

With Time · November 26, 2026 · 5 min read

On Wednesday, November 18, 2026, at 5:16 in the morning Eastern time, a spacecraft that left Earth before most people reading this were born crossed a line no human-made object had ever crossed. Voyager 1 — launched on September 5, 1977 — reached one light-day from Earth: 16.1 billion miles, or 25.9 billion kilometres, away. It is the farthest anything built by human hands has ever been from home.

A light-day is not a unit of time. It is a unit of distance — the distance light travels in 24 hours. Light moves at 186,000 miles a second, which adds up to about 16.09 billion miles a day. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, so every message between Earth and Voyager 1 now takes a full day in each direction. Say hello today, and the spacecraft hears it tomorrow. Its answer takes another day to come back. “If you want to say hello to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response,” said Suzanne Dodd, the mission’s project manager at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

The confirmation itself arrived a day late — because of the very distance it was confirming. Voyager 1 crossed the mark on Wednesday morning, but the signal carrying the news needed 24 hours to reach Earth, so engineers at JPL did not hear it until Thursday. And the signal they heard was almost nothing: not data, not pictures, not measurements — just the spacecraft’s carrier tone, a faint, steady hum that says I am still here. Voyager 1 has sent no science or engineering data since November 14. Working from that tone and from the spacecraft’s known trajectory, the team confirmed the milestone: one light-day, reached.

It took 49 years, two months and eleven days to get there. Voyager 1 left Earth on a pillar of fire in 1977, swung past Jupiter in 1979 and Saturn in 1980, and then kept going — out past the planets, out past the Sun’s own bubble of wind, the heliosphere, which it crossed in 2012, becoming the first human-made object in interstellar space. It travels now at about 38,000 miles an hour, roughly 17 kilometres a second, in the direction of a dim star called Gliese 445, more than 17 light-years away. It will pass within about 1.7 light-years of that star — in 40,000 years.

On board is the Golden Record, a twelve-inch disc of gold-plated copper carrying the sounds of Earth: greetings in fifty-five languages, a mother’s first words to her newborn child, the sound of rain, of a heartbeat, of a kiss — and music, from Bach to Chuck Berry. It was a message in a bottle, thrown into the cosmic ocean in the hope that someone, somewhere, someday, might find it. The bottle is now one light-day out, and still sailing.

People on Earth marked the moment. On the evening of November 17, NASA invited everyone to shine a golden light toward the sky — phones, flashlights, porch lights, whole buildings — a wave of light circling the planet for a spacecraft that can no longer see us. The Empire State Building glowed gold. It was a small gesture for a large distance: a hello that will take a full day to arrive.

Voyager 1 will not fly forever. Its power fades a little every year, and engineers expect it to fall silent sometime in the 2030s, its instruments switched off one by one to keep the last watt for the radio. Its twin, Voyager 2, is nine years behind it on the same road and will reach one light-day in November 2035. Until then, the farthest voice humanity has ever sent keeps moving away from us at 38,000 miles an hour — a machine built in the 1970s, outliving every expectation, carrying our greetings into a dark it will never come back from.

A message sent today arrives tomorrow. The reply comes the day after. Somewhere out there, 16.1 billion miles away, a small machine is still listening — and still, after all these years, still calling home.

“The Earth and Voyager are exactly 24 hours apart at the speed of light.” — Linda Spilker, Voyager project scientist
