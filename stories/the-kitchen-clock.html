Time Itself

The Kitchen Clock

It ticked two minutes fast for thirty years, and nobody in the house ever fixed it — because the whole family ran on its time.

With Time · November 9, 2026 · 4 min read

The kitchen clock was round, cream-colored, with black hands and a faint buzz that you only noticed when the house went quiet. It ran two minutes fast. Everyone knew. Nobody fixed it. To fix it would have meant admitting that the family's time was wrong, and the family's time was the only time that mattered.

Dinner was at six, by the kitchen clock, which was 5:58 by the rest of the world. The school bus was caught by the kitchen clock. Birthdays began two minutes early and ended two minutes late, which is the correct length of a birthday. Visitors would glance at it, frown, and say, 'Your clock is fast,' and someone in the family would always answer, 'No, it isn't,' with the calm of a person stating a fact about the weather.

Clocks are supposed to be honest. But a family's clock is not a measuring device; it is a member of the family. It was there when the children were small and the table was crowded. It was there when the children left and the table got quiet. It ticked through the loud years and the silent years with the same small buzz, two minutes ahead of the world, as if it knew something the world did not.

When the house was finally sold, the clock came down last. It had hung on that wall for thirty years, and the wall underneath was a pale square, like a photograph of a photograph. The new owners asked if it came with the house. It did not. It went to the eldest daughter's kitchen, where it still runs two minutes fast, and her children now eat dinner at six — which is 5:58 everywhere else.

That is how time works in a family. It is not kept. It is inherited.

“Every family has a clock that keeps its own time. It is never wrong. It is just keeping the family's time.”
